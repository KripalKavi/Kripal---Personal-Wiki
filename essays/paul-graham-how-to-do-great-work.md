# How to Do Great Work

## Metadata

**Author**: [Paul Graham](../entities/paul-graham.md)  
**Title**: "How to Do Great Work"  
**Year**: 2023  
**URL**: https://www.paulgraham.com/greatwork.html

---

## Summary

Graham argues that great work is not mainly the result of discipline, planning, or credentials. It comes from finding work at the intersection of natural aptitude, deep interest, and room for important contribution; then pushing to the frontier, noticing gaps, and exploring the promising ones with enough consistency, originality, and morale for compounding to take over.

The most PM-relevant insight is that choosing the right problem is the highest-leverage act. Execution matters, but originality in problem selection matters more: the best work often begins as a small, self-indulgent, unfashionable thread that later reveals a much larger frontier.

## Key Ideas

### The Four-Step Recipe

Graham reduces great work to a practical loop:

1. Choose a field.
2. Learn enough to get to the frontier.
3. Notice gaps.
4. Explore promising gaps.

Curiosity powers all four steps. It picks the field, sustains learning, makes anomalies visible, and supplies the energy to explore them when conventional incentives would point elsewhere.

### Choose Work by Interest + Aptitude, Not Prestige

The work should combine natural aptitude and deep interest. Graham explicitly warns that ambitious people are usually too conservative about whether an area has enough scope, so the bigger risk is not aiming too small in ambition but aiming too conventionally in topic.

The test is whether the field becomes more interesting as you learn more. If it does not, move. Fields do not deserve loyalty; the work does.

### Make Yourself a Big Target for Luck

Because you cannot know in advance which work will fit, Graham recommends trying many things, meeting people, reading broadly, asking questions, and following interestingness. This is not passive wandering. It is an active strategy for increasing exposure to useful accidents.

For product leaders, this reframes exploration as an input to judgment, not a distraction from execution.

### Great Work Starts Small and Evolves

Big work rarely begins as a fully formed plan. Graham's preferred pattern is to start with the simplest thing that could work, make successive versions, and let ambition increase through contact with the work and its audience.

This is especially important when building for people: get an early version in front of users, learn from their response, and evolve. Planning feels responsible, but in flexible media it often prevents discovery.

### Originality Lives in Problem Choice

Graham argues that people are often more original in solutions than in deciding what to work on. Fashion pulls smart people toward obvious problems, while overlooked problems sit in the shadow of assumptions that the field treats almost religiously.

Useful prompts:

- What are people in this field too attached to?
- What becomes possible if that assumption is discarded?
- If you took a break from "serious" work to follow a curiosity, what would you build?
- What seems wrong or missing now that everyone experienced has learned to ignore?

### Protect the Work Cycle

Hard work and relaxed thinking are a cycle. Deliberate work feeds the mind questions; walking, showering, and idle thought let those questions recombine. Distractions are dangerous because they steal not just focused work time, but the background mental slot where hard problems get solved.

This extends Graham's older "Top Idea in Your Mind" frame: attention is productive infrastructure.

### Morale Is Part of the System

Ambitious work depends on morale. Graham's practical advice is to treat setbacks as part of the search process, not evidence that the root goal is wrong. Backtrack as little as needed, switch to easier work when stuck to restart momentum, seek a small dedicated audience, work with energy-increasing people, and maintain the physical base of sleep, exercise, and walking.

For senior leaders, morale is not softness. It is an operating condition for ambitious judgment.

## Quotable Lines

> "The way to figure out what to work on is by working."

> "When in doubt, optimize for interestingness."

> "Don't let 'work' mean something other people tell you to do."

> "Curiosity is the best guide."

> "Original thinkers throw off new ideas about whatever they focus on."

> "The discoveries are out there, waiting to be made. Why not by you?"

## PM Application

1. **Treat problem selection as the senior PM craft.** For Copilot, Windows, M365, and Azure AI, the strategic bottleneck is less "can the team execute?" and more "are we working on the gap that matters?" Use Graham's four-step loop in strategy reviews: What frontier have we reached? What gap are we seeing that others ignore? Why is this the promising gap?

2. **Create a portfolio lane for unfashionable problems.** Graham's warning that unfashionable problems are undervalued is directly relevant to Microsoft, where planning systems tend to overweight visible competitive battles. Reserve capacity for problems that are not in the current hype cycle but become more interesting the closer the team gets.

3. **Use curiosity as a talent signal.** In PM hiring and growth conversations, ask what the person is excessively curious about, what tedious part of the domain they enjoy, and what question they keep returning to. That signal is harder to fake than generic strategic polish.

4. **Normalize small starts for large bets.** The essay pairs well with [Tiny Core](../concepts/tiny-core.md): start with the simplest thing that could work, then evolve through successive versions. At Microsoft scale, teams often skip the toy phase because it looks unserious. Graham's point is that toy-like early versions are often exactly how big ideas preserve discovery.

5. **Protect background cognition for senior leaders.** If a product leader's mind is filled with escalations, internal politics, and planning-process residue, the real strategy work is starved. Calendar design, walking time, and fewer attention-grabbing internal dramas are not productivity hacks; they are how hard product questions get solved.

6. **Audit org rituals for school-like passivity.** Graham attacks school systems for inducing passivity and test-hacking. Large companies can do the same through review rituals that reward deck performance over frontier exploration. The PM bar should be: did this team discover a real gap and learn something hard, or did it merely pass the quarterly test?
