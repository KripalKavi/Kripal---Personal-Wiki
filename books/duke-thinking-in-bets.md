# Thinking in Bets

**Author**: Annie Duke
**Year**: 2018
**Recommended by**: Stewart Butterfield (Lenny's Podcast, 2025-11-20) — cited as Annie Duke's first book, referenced as context for *Quit*

---

## Summary

Annie Duke applies the mental model of professional poker to decision-making in business and life. The core argument: most decisions are bets — you're choosing between uncertain outcomes, not certainties. The biggest obstacle to better decisions is *resulting* (judging past decisions by outcomes rather than by decision quality), which prevents learning and distorts future judgment. Thinking in bets means evaluating decisions on the quality of reasoning at the time, not on whether they turned out well.

---

## Key Ideas

### Resulting: The Enemy of Good Decision-Making
*Resulting* is the cognitive bias where we judge the quality of a past decision by its outcome. A good decision that turned out badly is retroactively called stupid. A bad decision that turned out well is called brilliant. Neither judgment is correct. The quality of a decision is determined by the quality of the reasoning and information at the time it was made — not by the outcome, which is partly random.

This matters enormously for organizations: if we punish bad outcomes and reward good outcomes regardless of decision quality, we teach people to make safe-looking decisions, avoid accountability for uncertainty, and dismiss luck's role in outcomes.

### Life Is Poker, Not Chess
Chess has complete information: you can see every piece. Poker has hidden information and probability. Most decisions in business and life are poker, not chess — incomplete information, uncertain outcomes, time pressure. Chess-player thinking (if I just think long enough, I'll find the right answer) fails in poker environments. Poker-player thinking (what's the best decision given what I can know? what's my expected value?) is the right mode.

### Calibrated Confidence
Rather than binary certainty ("I know this" / "I don't know this"), think in probabilities. "I'm 70% confident this product will hit target." "I'm 40% sure we have the right positioning." Forcing probabilistic language does two things: it makes reasoning explicit and testable, and it makes learning possible (if your 90% confidence calls are right only 60% of the time, you have a calibration problem).

### Seeking Out Disconfirmation
Most decision-makers build information systems that confirm their existing beliefs. Poker players have an incentive to seek disconfirmation — wrong beliefs lose chips. Duke recommends building the same incentive structure: actively seek out people and information that challenge your current view, not validate it.

### The Pre-Mortem
Before committing to a decision, run a pre-mortem: "It's 12 months from now and this initiative has failed spectacularly. What happened?" This technique surfaces failure modes that confirmation bias suppresses. It's easier to think of failure modes when you've assumed the failure, rather than having to argue against a plan everyone has bought into.

### Hindsight and Learning
Because we're susceptible to hindsight bias — "I knew it all along" — we systematically mis-learn from experience. We over-attribute failures to bad decisions and successes to skill. Duke's fix: record your predictions and reasoning at the time, then review them honestly when the outcome is known. The gap between prediction and outcome, stripped of hindsight, is the actual signal about your judgment.

## Quotable Lines

> "The quality of our lives is the sum of decision quality plus luck."

> "Resulting is judging a past decision by its outcome. It's the most common way we undermine our ability to learn."

> "Being wrong about something doesn't mean the decision was a mistake. Being right doesn't mean it wasn't."

> "Advance your opinion with 'I think' or 'I believe' or 'I'm not sure but' — it signals that a statement is belief, not fact."

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Resulting in post-mortems**: Most product post-mortems attribute success to good strategy and failure to bad execution or bad luck. Duke's framework says: evaluate the quality of the decision at the time it was made, given what was knowable. Was the decision to launch that feature well-reasoned, given the information available? That's the learning question. Outcome-based attribution distorts future decisions.

2. **Calibrated confidence in roadmap reviews**: Replace "I think this will work" with "I'm 60% confident this activation approach will move the needle." The probability estimate is less important than the discipline of making uncertainty explicit. It also makes reviews more honest — people stop performing certainty they don't feel.

3. **Pre-mortems before major launches**: Before any significant Copilot feature launch, run a pre-mortem. "It's Q3 and this feature is the failure of the quarter. What happened?" This surfaces underweighted risks in a safe context, before the team is too committed to hear skeptics.

4. **Seeking disconfirmation from engineering and design partners**: Product leaders at large companies tend to hear most readily from people who agree with them. Deliberately assign someone the role of finding the strongest case against your proposed direction. This is the poker player's practice applied to roadmap decisions.

5. **Recording predictions before launches**: Keep a written record of what you predicted before major launches — target metrics, adoption assumptions, user behavior hypotheses. Review them honestly 6 months later. This is the antidote to hindsight bias and the best source of calibration data about your own forecasting accuracy.
