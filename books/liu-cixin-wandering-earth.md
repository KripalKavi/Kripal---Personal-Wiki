# The Wandering Earth (and Other Stories)

**Author**: Liu Cixin
**Year**: Original Chinese publications 2000–2010s; English translations ~2017–2020
**Recommended by**: Boris Cherny (Lenny's Podcast, 2026-02-19) — preferred over *Three Body Problem*: "he just has some really, really amazing stories... quite interesting to see Chinese sci-fi, because it has a very different perspective"

---

## Summary

A short story collection by Liu Cixin, the Chinese science fiction author best known for *The Three-Body Problem*. Boris recommends this over the novel trilogy — the short stories are where Liu Cixin's range and originality are most visible. The title story involves moving the entire Earth out of the solar system; other stories explore compressed time, dark forest competitive dynamics, and the relationship between human civilization and cosmic-scale forces. Boris recommends it specifically for its *non-western* perspective on technology and civilization.

---

## Key Ideas

### The Wandering Earth (title story)
Humanity discovers the Sun is going to expand and render Earth uninhabitable. The solution: attach enormous fusion engines to the Earth and slowly push it out of the solar system on a 2,500-year journey to a new star. The story is told from the perspective of ordinary people living through this multigenerational project — the collective sacrifice, the loss of seasons, the generational memory of a sky with stars.

The distinctively Chinese framing: collective civilization survival over individual flourishing. Not "let's build spaceships and escape," but "we take the Earth with us, at enormous cost, because that's our home." Western sci-fi would have told a different story.

### Non-Western Frameworks for Technology
Boris's most pointed observation: "it's also just quite interesting to see Chinese sci-fi, because it has a very different perspective than Western sci-fi, and the way that, at least, he, as a writer, thinks about it." Liu Cixin's stories consistently operate at civilizational scale, with collective actors, and with a darker view of competitive dynamics (see: Dark Forest hypothesis from the novels).

The cognitive value: reading this recalibrates intuitions about AI development that are often framed through a Silicon Valley or Western liberal lens. Chinese AI development institutions, policy frameworks, and product strategies are operating from a different set of assumptions — understanding the fictional imagination helps understand the strategic imagination.

### Compressed Time and the Long View
Several Liu Cixin stories involve radically compressed or expanded time scales — civilizations that operate at different temporal frequencies, decisions that play out over millennia. Boris reads this during a period of living in rural Japan, thinking in "long time skills" governed by rice planting seasons and miso fermentation timelines. The counterpoint to the Accelerando reading: both are about exponential change, but Liu Cixin adds the dimension of patience and civilizational continuity.

### The Mountain
One of the shorter stories in the collection: an alien species has evolved on the surface of a gas giant with no concept of astronomy. The story is their discovery that "the universe" (what they call "the mountain") is larger than their world. A meditation on the limits of perception when your evolutionary context doesn't prepare you for the scale of what exists.

Relevant analog: most organizations' "universe" of AI possibility is bounded by what they've seen in the past 18 months. The mountain is larger.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **The non-western framing is strategically useful**: Microsoft competes with Alibaba Cloud, Baidu, and ByteDance's AI stack in global markets, and Copilot's positioning will be evaluated by customers and governments with non-Western frameworks. Understanding how Chinese technologists and scientists imagine the AI future — including through fiction — gives signal about the competitive landscape that Western analyst reports don't provide.

2. **Civilizational-scale thinking as a leadership practice**: Liu Cixin's consistent frame is: what does this decision look like at the level of civilization, across hundreds of years? Most quarterly planning doesn't require this. But for decisions about AI safety, data sovereignty, model access policy, and long-term platform bets, this temporal scale is arguably the appropriate one. Worth having a mental habit of occasionally asking: what does this look like in 100 years?

3. **The collective vs. individual axis**: The Wandering Earth's premise — we move the Earth together, at enormous collective sacrifice, rather than build individual escape vehicles — maps onto a real policy and product design tension in AI: individual optimization vs. collective welfare. This is the core tension in Microsoft's AI governance, Copilot design for enterprise vs. personal use, and the regulatory conversations Microsoft participates in.

4. **Pairing with Accelerando**: Boris recommends both books in the same lightning round answer. They make a complementary pair: Accelerando provides the Western singularity mental model (acceleration, individual actors, cognitive explosion); Liu Cixin provides the Chinese civilizational model (long time horizons, collective actors, dark-forest competitive dynamics). Together they cover more of the actual strategic landscape than either alone.
