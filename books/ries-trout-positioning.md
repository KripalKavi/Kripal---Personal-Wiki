# Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind

**Author**: Al Ries & Jack Trout
**Year**: 1981
**Recommended by**: Stewart Butterfield (Lenny's Podcast, 2025-11-20) — cited as the foundational reference for "We Don't Sell Saddles Here"

---

## Summary

The classic text that defined product positioning as a discipline. Ries and Trout's central argument: marketing battles are not fought in the marketplace — they're fought in the mind of the prospect. You can't create new ideas in someone's head; you can only reposition existing ones. The book that shifted how a generation of product and marketing leaders thought about what they were actually competing for.

---

## Key Ideas

### The Mind Is the Battlefield
Products don't win on features, quality, or price. They win by owning a concept in the prospect's mind. The mind resists change — once a position is established, it's extremely durable. Trying to dislodge the leader with a "better product" argument almost never works because the mind filters out information that conflicts with what it already believes.

### You Can't Create a New Idea
Almost impossible to put a completely new idea in someone's mind. Far easier to take two existing ideas they already hold and combine them. "It's Jaws meets Star Wars" — two existing concepts, instantly imaginable. This is why positioning statements that use analogies ("it's Slack for lawyers" or "it's Netflix for skills training") are effective: they don't require new mental models.

### Ladders of the Mind
The mind has a limited hierarchy of "rungs" per product category. The top rung is extremely hard to displace. Usually only the top two brands in a category are remembered and considered. Implication: if you're not #1 or #2, you need to reframe the category, not compete within it.

### Repositioning the Competition
One of the more aggressive tactics: you can create room in the mind by repositioning the competitor rather than just positioning yourself. "Rental car companies try harder" (Avis on Hertz) — the implicit message is that the leader is complacent. This creates a category where Avis's "trying harder" is the relevant differentiator.

### The Brand Extension Trap
One of the book's warnings: don't dilute your position by extending the brand into unrelated categories. When your brand name means something specific in the mind, putting it on a different product confuses that meaning. Ries & Trout were ahead of their time — this argument plays out repeatedly in tech (trying to extend a B2B brand into consumer, or a consumer brand into enterprise).

### "Not What You Think It Is" Positioning
Sometimes the best position isn't about what your product does — it's about what category your product reframes. Butterfield's "We Don't Sell Saddles Here" application: Slack isn't messaging software; it's a frictionless, connected team experience. The latter is a position in the mind; the former is a product description.

## Quotable Lines

> "Positioning is not what you do to a product. It is what you do to the mind of the prospect."

> "The basic approach of positioning is not to create something new and different, but to manipulate what's already up there in the mind."

> "In our overcommunicated society, the paradox is that nothing is more important than communication, yet there is less and less of it each year."

> "The mind, as a defense against the volume of today's communications, screens and rejects much of the information offered it."

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Butterfield's direct citation**: He quoted *Positioning* specifically in the context of "We Don't Sell Saddles Here" — if your product is different from alternatives in any significant way, you're not just building the product, you're building the category. Those tasks are inseparable. For Copilot: what category is Copilot actually defining, and what does that mean for how Microsoft talks about it?

2. **The mind is the battlefield for Copilot**: Users come to Copilot with a pre-existing mental model ("it's ChatGPT but in Office"). That's the position Microsoft is working with. Competing with "we have better AI" or "we have more features" tries to win with a better product in a category the user has already formed. The more effective play is repositioning: change what category Copilot occupies.

3. **Ladders of the mind and the ChatGPT problem**: In the category "AI assistant," ChatGPT owns the top rung for most users. Microsoft cannot dislodge this with "Copilot is also good." The Ries & Trout playbook says: reframe the category. Copilot's position is enterprise-integrated, context-aware, security-compliant AI work assistant — a different category from general AI chatbots. Competing in the "chatbot" category is the wrong ladder.

4. **Brand extension caution for Copilot breadth**: Ries & Trout warn against brand extension diluting meaning. "Copilot" now means: GitHub Copilot, M365 Copilot, Copilot+ PC, Copilot in Azure, Copilot in Dynamics. Each is a slightly different value proposition. The risk: users don't know what "Copilot" means, which is the opposite of having a position.

5. **"Two existing ideas" for new Copilot features**: When introducing a new Copilot capability, position it using two existing concepts the user already holds. Don't describe what it technically does — describe which two things they already know it combines.
