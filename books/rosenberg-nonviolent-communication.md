# Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life

**Author**: Marshall B. Rosenberg
**Year**: 1999 (3rd ed. 2015)
**Recommended by**: Rachel Lockett (Lenny's Podcast, 2025-11-23) — foundational source for the NVC framework she teaches

---

## Summary

Marshall Rosenberg's foundational text on a framework for communication that focuses on mutual understanding rather than coercion, judgment, or manipulation. NVC holds that most communication breakdowns arise from language that evaluates, judges, or demands — rather than expresses genuine needs. The four-step process (Observations → Feelings → Needs → Requests) creates a pathway through conflict that keeps both parties out of defensiveness.

---

## Key Ideas

### The Root Cause of Communication Failure
Rosenberg's diagnosis: most conflict in human relationships comes not from malicious intent but from language patterns that we learned without questioning. "You always do this." "I need you to stop." "You're being defensive." Each of these sentences makes claims about the other person rather than expressing what the speaker is experiencing. That structure triggers defensiveness automatically.

### The Four Components
1. **Observations**: Describe what you observe factually — what you see, hear, or recall — without evaluation. Not "you're always late" (evaluation) but "I noticed you arrived 20 minutes after the meeting started" (observation). Mixing observations with evaluations is the most common communication error.

2. **Feelings**: Express your emotional state, not your interpretation of the other person's behavior. "I feel frustrated" is a feeling. "I feel that you don't respect my time" is a thought about the other person dressed up as a feeling. The distinction matters: feelings invite empathy; interpretations invite argument.

3. **Needs**: Connect feelings to underlying universal human needs (safety, respect, connection, autonomy, fairness). "When I hear X, I feel Y, because I need Z." This structure removes blame: your need is yours, not caused by the other person's behavior. It opens space for the other person to understand what's at stake for you.

4. **Requests**: A specific, doable request for an action that would meet the need. Not "I need you to be more respectful" (demand disguised as request, and not specific) but "Would you be willing to reply to my messages within 24 hours?" The request should be concrete, present-tense, and achievable.

### Empathic Listening
The companion to NVC expression is NVC listening: hearing others' observations, feelings, needs, and requests beneath what they actually say. When someone says "you never listen to me," they're expressing a feeling (frustration) and a need (to be heard). The NVC-trained listener responds to the underlying need, not the accusation.

### Connection Before Solutions
Most managers jump to problem-solving before the person feels heard. NVC insists on connection first: the need to be understood has to be met before any solution will land. Trying to solve a problem that the other person doesn't yet feel you understand is like offering directions to someone who hasn't told you where they're trying to go.

### "Stay on Your Side of the Net"
The Stanford Touchy Feely framing (Carol Robbins) that Lockett uses: your job is to own your observations, feelings, and needs — not to characterize the other person's intentions or behavior. The moment you cross the net and describe what the other person is doing (rather than what you're experiencing), defensiveness follows. The net is the line between your experience and your interpretation of theirs.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Cross-functional conflict is where NVC earns its keep**: The hardest conversations at Microsoft are typically between product and engineering ("you don't understand the tech"), product and legal ("you're always blocking us"), or product and leadership ("the strategy isn't clear"). These conversations get stuck in evaluation language. NVC moves them to: "When I hear [this decision], I feel [uncertain], because I need [clarity on the principle]. Would you be willing to [walk me through the reasoning]?"

2. **The "I feel that you..." trap in 1:1s**: Most performance feedback breaks down because it crosses the net. "I feel that you're not engaged in the roadmap process" is actually a judgment. "When I see you leave roadmap reviews early, I feel concerned, because I need our team to have shared context. Would you be willing to stay for the full session?" — this is the NVC version. Same message, completely different receptivity.

3. **"Stay on your side of the net" as a coaching reminder**: When giving feedback to PMs who come to you with interpersonal conflicts, use this phrase. Help them separate "what happened" (observable) from "what it means about the other person" (interpretation). The distinction usually reveals they have a legitimate need that they've been communicating as an accusation.

4. **Connection before solutions in difficult 1:1s**: Before problem-solving in a 1:1 where someone is frustrated or disengaged, spend time on the feelings and needs layer. Not because it's nice — because solution suggestions fall flat until the person feels understood.
