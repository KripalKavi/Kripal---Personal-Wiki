# Reboot: Leadership and the Art of Growing Up

**Author**: Jerry Colonna
**Year**: 2019
**Recommended by**: Rachel Lockett (Lenny's Podcast, 2025-11-23) — cited as the source of the most important question in Lockett's coaching practice

---

## Summary

Jerry Colonna, executive coach and former VC partner, argues that the most important work a leader can do is not strategy, execution, or culture-building — it's growing up. The question at the heart of the book: "How are you complicit in creating the conditions you claim you don't want?" Most leadership dysfunction traces back to unexamined beliefs, fear-based behavior, and patterns inherited from childhood. Reboot is an invitation to look inward with rigor, not just optimize outward behavior.

---

## Key Ideas

### The Central Question
> "How are you complicit in creating the conditions you claim you don't want?"

This is Colonna's defining contribution to the leadership canon. It applies to every persistent problem: a team that doesn't take initiative, a co-founder relationship that's deteriorating, a culture of over-work, a pattern of escalations. If the same dysfunction persists despite your efforts to fix it, you are a variable in the equation. The question doesn't assign blame — it opens an inquiry. What in your behavior, your communication, your expectations, or your emotional responses is reinforcing the pattern?

### Leaders Bring Their Whole Selves
Colonna's foundational premise: you don't leave who you are at home when you become a leader. The fears, the need for approval, the impulse to control, the difficulty with vulnerability — all of it arrives with you in the boardroom, the 1:1, the all-hands. Pretending otherwise doesn't make it less true. It just makes it invisible and therefore more powerful.

### Growing Up vs. Growing Out
Most leadership development focuses on growing out — acquiring new skills, tools, and behaviors. Colonna focuses on growing up — developing the self-awareness to see patterns, the emotional regulation to not be driven by fear, and the clarity to know what actually matters. Growing out without growing up produces leaders who are technically competent and personally destructive.

### Radical Self-Inquiry
The book is structured around a series of questions Colonna uses with coaching clients, paired with his own story. The questions are genuinely uncomfortable: What are you afraid of? What do you believe about yourself that might not be true? What would you do if you weren't afraid? The inquiry isn't therapeutic indulgence — it's how leaders stop projecting unexamined fears onto their teams and organizations.

### The Scared Kid in the Boardroom
Colonna's metaphor: many destructive leadership behaviors are the scared child's strategies applied in an adult context. The kid who learned that control = safety becomes the micromanaging executive. The kid who learned that being needed = love becomes the heroic martyr leader who never delegates. Recognizing the pattern doesn't make it disappear, but it's the prerequisite for changing it.

### Stillness as Leadership Practice
Colonna argues for practices that create internal stillness — meditation, journaling, therapy, physical exertion. Not as productivity hacks, but as the mechanism by which leaders develop the capacity to respond rather than react. The fastest-moving leaders often need more stillness, not less.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **The complicity question for persistent team dysfunction**: If the same problem keeps recurring — slow decisions, initiative deficit, excessive escalations, team disengagement — Colonna's question is the right diagnostic. What in your behavior as the leader is maintaining that condition? This isn't self-blame; it's the most actionable question because it's the only variable you fully control.

2. **The heroic martyr pattern in PM leadership**: One of the most common destructive patterns for high-performing PMs who become leaders: continuing to solve problems yourself rather than building the team's capability to solve them. Colonna's framing: this is usually about a need to be needed, not about organizational effectiveness. The fix requires inner inquiry, not just behavioral training.

3. **Applying this before major reorgs or team restructures**: Leaders often reorg to fix people problems that are actually leadership pattern problems. A different org structure with the same leader will reproduce the same dysfunction. Colonna's work suggests: run the inquiry before designing the fix.

4. **The "scared kid" lens for difficult directs**: When a direct report's behavior is consistently frustrating or confusing, Colonna's frame is useful: what is this person afraid of? What strategy are they using to feel safe? The answer often explains the behavior and points to a more effective management approach than direct correction.

5. **Stillness practices for a Senior Director**: At a role where the meeting load, the email volume, and the strategic complexity all compound simultaneously, Colonna's argument for stillness isn't soft. The Louisville educator study (Richie Davidson) provides empirical support: 5 minutes/day of attention practice measurably improves outcomes for the people in your proximity.
