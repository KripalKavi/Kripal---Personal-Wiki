# Seeing Like a State

**Author**: James C. Scott
**Year**: 1998
**Recommended by**: [Max Schoening](../entities/max-schoening.md) (Lenny's Podcast, 2026-05-03) — "I give mostly to executives that I think are creating a lot of systems"

---

## Why Schoening Recommends It

Schoening's framing is precise and applies directly to executive product leadership:

> "There is a famous Stack Overflow that popularized this, but it's the idea of — are you actually just designing a system so that you have legibility, but the way that you've created that legibility completely neglects the reality of the system on the ground? So I think of it as: great, you're the executive, you have these status reports, and you think you know exactly how your teams work. If you actually spend time with the teams, you would realize that none of that is actually true. Executives love creating fake legibility for themselves because we don't like noise as humans. We want the signal, but there's often less signal in it than one might think."

The book is the canonical articulation of how **top-down systems impose legibility that obliterates the real, working knowledge on the ground** — with destructive results when the system optimizes against its own fake map.

---

## Summary

Scott (Yale political scientist) examines 20th-century state-driven schemes — scientific forestry, planned cities (Brasília), collectivized agriculture (USSR), villagization (Tanzania) — that share a structure: high-modernist confidence that complex local realities can be reorganized around a top-down legible plan. Each fails, sometimes catastrophically. Scott extracts the failure mechanism:

1. **The state needs legibility** — to tax, conscript, surveil, plan. Legibility means: a uniform, gridded, quantifiable representation of the territory.
2. **Real complexity is illegible** to that representation. Forests have biodiversity; villages have customary land rights; agriculture has weather, soil, local technique.
3. **High-modernist ideology** assumes the legible representation *is* the real thing, or that the real thing should be reorganized to match.
4. **The state imposes the representation** — replants forests as monocultures, redraws property lines, collectivizes farms, builds Brasília's modernist plan.
5. **The legible system fails** because it lacks the *mētis* (Scott's term for local, embedded, practical knowledge) that made the real system work.

The destruction is not from malice but from the *gap between map and territory* — and from the structural inability of the state to value what isn't on its map.

## Key Concepts

### Legibility

The transformation of complex, varied, local reality into uniform categories the central authority can process. Examples: surnames (none in medieval villages — imposed for tax collection), standardized weights and measures, gridded streets, official census categories. Legibility is a *prerequisite* for centralized administration but a *destroyer* of local variation.

### High Modernism

The ideology that science + state planning can rationalize human society. Le Corbusier's "city of tomorrow" is the canonical example. High modernism is not inherently authoritarian, but combined with state power and the discrediting of local knowledge, it produces destruction.

### Mētis

Local, embodied, practical knowledge that cannot be codified into rules. The farmer who knows when the soil is ready, the sailor who reads the wind, the village mediator who knows the families. Mētis is the actual operating knowledge of any complex system. The high-modernist state cannot see it, can't be transmitted in reports, doesn't fit the legibility grid — and is therefore systematically destroyed when the state imposes its plan.

### The Four-Step Failure Pattern

1. Administrative simplification of complex reality
2. High-modernist confidence in the simplified model
3. Authoritarian power to impose
4. Civil society too weak to resist

When all four are present, the failure is catastrophic. When any are missing, the failure is partial.

## Why Executives Need This Book

Schoening's framing — *"executives love creating fake legibility for themselves"* — applies a state-craft failure to corporate leadership. The mechanism is identical:

1. **The executive needs legibility** — to make decisions across thousands of people, the exec needs reports, dashboards, OKRs, headcount counts, status updates.
2. **Real work is illegible to those instruments.** What teams actually do, the trust networks, the customer rituals, the workarounds, the implicit priorities — none of it shows up in the report.
3. **High-modernist management** assumes the dashboard *is* the work, or wants to reshape the work to fit the dashboard.
4. **The exec imposes** new metrics, new structures, new processes — usually with the best intentions.
5. **The work degrades** because the imposition optimized against fake legibility and destroyed the mētis.

The pattern recurs constantly in tech companies: every "metrics-driven" reorg that destroys the team that was actually working. Every dashboard that becomes the goal. Every OKR cascade that misaligns the org with the customer.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Audit your dashboards for fake legibility.** What does your status report tell you about how the team operates? How much of it is actually mētis-free (data points that exist independent of how the team actually works)? If most of the report is generated *for* the report, the report is fake legibility. Spend time on the ground regularly to keep your model honest.

2. **Be suspicious of "data-driven" simplifications that destroy mētis.** Every "let's standardize" / "let's centralize" / "let's measure" intervention has a fake-legibility risk. Ask: what *embedded knowledge* will this displace? Sometimes the answer is "nothing valuable" — sometimes the answer is "the thing that made this team work."

3. **The high-modernist Copilot risk.** Microsoft's tendency to build very legible top-down systems (compliance frameworks, enterprise admin, audit logs, RBAC) is correct for many enterprise use cases. But Copilot inside individual users' workflows can become Scott's state imposing its grid on the village — administering away the practical knowledge of how people actually use M365. Build Copilot to amplify mētis, not replace it.

4. **The exec is in the village, not on the hill.** The book argues for executives spending time inside the work, not just receiving reports about it. The Schoening / Lenny interpretation is the same: *"If you actually spend time with the teams, you would realize that none of that is actually true."* The cost of skipping this is years of well-intentioned wrong decisions.

5. **Pair with [Hyper-Realistic Work-Like Activities](../concepts/hyper-realistic-work-like-activities.md).** Stewart Butterfield's framework names the symptom (work-looking activities that produce no value); Scott names the root cause (fake legibility making the org optimize for representations rather than reality).

6. **The cultural risk: managers as state agents.** PM managers may become unwitting agents of the legibility regime — generating reports the exec wants, optimizing the team for what's measured, suppressing mētis-flavored work that's hard to summarize. Push back on this in your own management chain. The senior PM job is to keep the team in contact with the actual work.
