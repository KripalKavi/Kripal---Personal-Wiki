# Nonviolent Communication (NVC)

---

## Definition

A communication framework for navigating conflict and difficult conversations, developed by psychologist Marshall Rosenberg. The core premise: most interpersonal conflict escalates because people conflate observations with interpretations, feelings with judgments, and needs with demands.

NVC provides a four-step structure that separates these categories, enabling conversations where both people feel heard rather than attacked.

The four steps:
1. **Observations**: What happened, stated factually — what a camera would record. No interpretation, no characterization.
2. **Feelings**: Your actual emotional experience. Not "I feel *that you*..." (which is a judgment) — but "I feel anxious," "I feel dismissed," "I feel frustrated."
3. **Needs**: The universal human needs at stake — connection, recognition, fairness, respect, clarity, safety. Stated without blame. Everyone has these needs; acknowledging them humanizes both parties.
4. **Request**: A small, specific, achievable ask. Not a demand (which triggers defensiveness). Not "I need you to change." But "Could we find 15 minutes this week to align on this?"

---

## The "Stay on Your Side of the Net" Rule

From Stanford's Touchy Feely course (credited to Carol Robbins). The most practical heuristic from NVC:

Your job in a difficult conversation is to own:
- What **you** observed (factually)
- What **you** felt
- What **you** need

The moment you cross the net — characterizing the other person's intentions, behavior, or motivations — you trigger defensiveness and the conversation closes. "You always dismiss my ideas in meetings" is a cross-net statement. "In the last three planning meetings I shared an idea and didn't get a response — I felt ignored and I'm not sure if my input is valued" stays on your side of the net.

The discipline is hard because crossing the net feels natural — you feel that you *know* what the other person meant. But even when you're right about their intent, stating it closes the conversation. Stay on your side.

---

## Why It Matters

Most people approach conflict trying to win or to convince. NVC reframes the goal: **mutual understanding**, not victory.

This reframe is not soft — it's strategic. When both parties feel understood, they become willing to problem-solve. When either party feels attacked, they defend, and nothing moves.

The conflict reframe from Rachel Lockett: *"When we are in conflict, something important is at stake."* Conflict isn't a problem to avoid — it's a signal that something important to at least one person isn't being honored. Leaning in with NVC is how you get to what that thing actually is.

Conscious Leadership parallel: Jerry Colonna's question — "How are you complicit in creating the conditions you claim you don't want?" — is the NVC mindset applied to persistent dynamics. If you're in the same conflict repeatedly, you're a variable in the equation.

## The Four Steps in Practice

**Without NVC (common pattern)**:
> "You always shoot down my ideas in front of the team. It's really demoralizing and I don't understand why you do it."

Problems: "always" (overgeneralization), "shoot down" (characterization), "I don't understand why" (attribution of motive). The other person is now defending their character, not solving a problem.

**With NVC**:
> "In the last three sprint reviews, I've shared proposals and heard silence — I felt unsure whether my ideas were landing, and I need to feel like there's a space to develop them together. Could we find 30 minutes to walk through the roadmap section before the next review?"

What changed: specific observable event, owned feeling, named need, concrete request. The other person can respond to this without defending.

---

## Common Failure Modes

- **"I feel that you..."** — This is a judgment masquerading as a feeling. If you can replace "I feel" with "I think," you've crossed the net.
- **The demand disguised as a request**: "I need you to stop doing X" is a demand. A request is something the other person can reasonably decline.
- **Skipping needs**: Moving directly from feelings to requests without naming the underlying need makes the request feel arbitrary. Naming the need ("I need predictability") makes the request make sense.
- **Observations that aren't observable**: "You were dismissive" is an interpretation. "You looked at your phone three times while I was presenting" is an observation.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Cross-functional conflict with engineering**: The most common escalation pattern — PM feels engineering is blocking, engineering feels PM doesn't understand technical constraints. NVC provides the template: "In the last two sprints, three features I prioritized were descoped after estimation — I'm feeling uncertain about our shared roadmap commitment, and I need us to have a shared understanding of what's in scope. Can we find time this week to realign?" This is solvable. "Engineering keeps undercutting the roadmap" is not a productive opening.

2. **Difficult feedback conversations**: NVC is the right structure for feedback that could trigger defensiveness. Lead with the specific observation (what you saw, not what you concluded), own your reaction, name the need (high performance, team trust, execution quality), make a specific request (change X, try Y).

3. **1:1s where something is clearly wrong**: When a direct report says "I'm fine" but clearly isn't — use Level 3 listening to notice what's beneath the words, then use NVC framing to create a space. "I've noticed you seem quieter in the last few team meetings [observation]. I want to make sure things are okay [need for connection]. Is there anything you'd want to share?" The non-invasive format makes it easier to open up.

4. **Training your PM team**: Teaching NVC concepts to your PMs improves the quality of their stakeholder relationships at scale. A PM who can navigate conflict without escalating saves their Senior Director significant overhead. Consider running a team session on "stay on your side of the net" — it's immediately applicable.

5. **Skip-level conversations with frustrated teams**: When a team is unhappy with a decision (reorganization, strategy change, resource cut), NVC on the leadership side creates the safety for honest conversation. Acknowledge what happened specifically, acknowledge the impact (don't minimize), name the business need, make a specific invitation for input. Contrast with the defensive "we made this decision for good reasons" posture that shuts down trust.
