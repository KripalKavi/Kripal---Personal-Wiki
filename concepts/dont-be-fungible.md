# Don't Be Fungible

---

## Definition

A career durability principle for the AI era, attributed to economist Larry Summers and developed by Marc Andreessen: **don't be replaceable**. Don't just be one thing.

AI will commoditize any skill that can be described in general, isolated terms. The durable career position is an **unusual combination** of genuine depth in multiple domains — a combination so specific that no single AI prompt and no generalist human can replicate it.

---

## Why It Matters

In the AI era, the threat isn't that your job disappears — it's that your *specific task bundle* becomes replicable. A PM who is "good at requirements, roadmaps, and stakeholder alignment" is fungible because those tasks can increasingly be described and delegated. A PM who has deep expertise in enterprise security *and* strong AI evaluation skills *and* long-standing relationships in a specific vertical is not fungible — the combination is too rare.

The key insight from Andreessen: **the additive effect of being good at two things is more than double. Being good at three things is more than triple.** You become a super-relevant specialist in the *combination* of domains. The domains themselves may be individually common; the combination is rare.

---

## The Scott Adams Illustration

Scott Adams (creator of Dilbert) articulated this principle as career advice before Andreessen popularized it:

> "I could have been a pretty good cartoonist, or I could have been pretty good at business. But the fact that I was a cartoonist who understood business made me spectacularly great at making Dilbert."

The best cartoonist in the world without business understanding couldn't have made Dilbert. The best business person without cartooning skill couldn't have made Dilbert. The combination is what created the category-defining work.

**Hollywood auteur parallel**: The superstars in film aren't the best directors or the best writers — they're the people who can do both. Those people (auteurs) are the ones who actually move the field.

---

## The E/F-Shaped Career

A practical model for building non-fungible skill combinations:

**T-shaped** (old model): Deep expertise in one domain, broad awareness of many others.

**E/F-shaped** (updated model for AI era): Genuine working capability in 2–3 domains, with deep expertise in at least one.

Visualize the letter E lying on its side:
- The **vertical** = how deep you go in your primary domain (if coding: all the way to assembly; if PM: deep domain expertise in a specific vertical)
- The **horizontal bars** = additional domains where you can function with AI as your tool (a coder who can now PM and design with AI; a PM who can code and design with AI)

The E/F shape matters because:
1. You can evaluate the AI's output in each domain (you understand enough to know when it's wrong)
2. The combination creates perspective that AI cannot replicate
3. You become the bridge between domains — the person who can see the whole problem

---

## Examples

**Fungible**: "I'm a PM who knows how to run sprints, write PRDs, and communicate with stakeholders" — this is a general description; it can be approximated by AI or any experienced generalist PM.

**Non-fungible examples**:
- PM + deep AI/ML evaluation fluency + enterprise security domain knowledge
- Engineer + strong product taste + user research skills
- Designer + understanding of full stack architecture + content strategy expertise
- The Dilbert combination: cartoonist + business understanding
- The auteur combination: director + writer + sometimes actor

**Non-fungible at Microsoft specifically**: PM expertise + genuine depth in one Microsoft vertical (healthcare, education, legal, manufacturing, financial services) + AI product evaluation skills = a combination very few people in the world possess

---

## AI Dramatically Lowers the Barrier

The E/F-shaped career used to require years to develop each additional arm. AI changes this:

1. **AI as teacher**: Ask AI to train you in an adjacent domain. "I'm a PM, train me to evaluate AI model outputs." "I'm an engineer, teach me to think about user experience." It will give you assignments, evaluate your results, explain concepts at your level.

2. **AI as capability amplifier**: You don't need to be an expert-level coder to use coding as a meaningful arm of your E/F. You need to be good enough to direct AI coding and evaluate what it gives you. AI has dramatically lowered the competence threshold for each arm.

3. **"Every spare hour should be spent asking AI to train you"** — Andreessen's specific advice. Use AI for learning as aggressively as you use it for work output.

---

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Audit your own fungibility**: Can your value proposition be described in general PM terms? If yes, that's the part most at risk. What combination of specific expertise is genuinely yours? Build toward the combination, not just PM competence.

2. **The Microsoft-specific non-fungible combinations**:
   - Microsoft 365 / Copilot expertise + AI evaluation skills + education vertical knowledge
   - Azure AI platform depth + enterprise security understanding + specific industry domain
   - GitHub Copilot expertise + developer experience depth + AI safety/quality evaluation
   These are combinations very few people have. Building toward them creates a durable position.

3. **Develop your team toward E/F-shaped profiles**: When coaching PMs, help them identify their second and third arms. The PM who can also deeply understand data analytics, or who develops genuine AI evaluation skills, or who becomes the go-to expert in a specific customer vertical — those are the durable careers on your team.

4. **Apply it in hiring**: Look for the Scott Adams combination. Two candidates: one who is deeply experienced in general PM skills, one who is moderately experienced in PM but deeply expert in healthcare AI or enterprise compliance. The second is more valuable and harder to replace.

5. **Task loss reframe for team planning**: The question for your team isn't "which roles will AI replace?" It's "which task bundles are becoming fungible?" Identify those tasks, reassign them to AI, and redirect human effort toward the non-fungible combination work: domain judgment, cross-functional synthesis, relationship-based insight.
