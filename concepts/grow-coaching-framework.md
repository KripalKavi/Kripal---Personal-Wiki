# GROW Coaching Framework

---

## Definition

A structured methodology for coaching conversations — guiding someone to their own solution rather than telling them what to do. Originally developed by Sir John Whitmore; widely used in executive coaching and high-performance team development.

The four stages:
- **G — Goal**: What do you want to achieve? What does success look like? (The actual desired state, not the surface version)
- **R — Reality**: What is the current situation? What's actually happening right now?
- **O — Options**: What could you do? What are all the possible approaches?
- **W — Will / Way Forward**: What will you do? What's your specific commitment and next step?

---

## Prerequisite: Level 3 Listening

GROW only works when the coach is operating at **Level 3 (global) listening** — attending to what's beneath the words: tone, body language, emotional subtext, what isn't being said.

- **Level 1 — Internal**: Your attention is on your own thoughts, preparing your response. "People don't listen; they just wait for their turn to speak."
- **Level 2 — Focused**: Tracking the words and understanding what's said. Most "good listeners" operate here.
- **Level 3 — Global**: Attending to the full experience — the gap between what's said and what's actually happening. This is the only level where real coaching happens.

If you're thinking about your next question while the person is still talking, you're at Level 1. GROW questions asked from Level 1 feel interrogative. Asked from Level 3, they feel supportive. The difference is entirely in the quality of attention.

---

## Why It Matters

The instinct when someone brings a problem to you is to solve it — especially if you have domain knowledge and a strong opinion. This is directing, not coaching, and it has a hidden cost: the person learns the answer, not the thinking. Over time, a team whose manager always solves the problem becomes dependent, less capable, and slows down.

GROW is a tool for resisting that instinct systematically. It forces the person to do the thinking, which builds capability and accountability simultaneously. The manager's job in a GROW conversation is to ask good questions, not provide answers.

The counterintuitive part: GROW conversations often produce the *same answer* the manager would have given — but because the person reached it themselves, they own it and execute on it with more commitment.

## Live Demonstration: Lenny's Newsletter Overwhelm

Rachel Lockett ran a live GROW session with Lenny on the podcast about his relationship with his newsletter — demonstrating each stage in real time:

**Goal**: What does Lenny actually want? Not "less stress" (surface) but the real desired state. Through questioning, the goal becomes clearer: a sustainable creative relationship with the newsletter rather than a performance obligation.

**Reality**: Lenny describes the experience as the Indiana Jones boulder — a giant boulder permanently chasing him. He can't stop running. The newsletter never feels done. This is the real current state, uncovered by genuine listening rather than a checklist question.

**Options**: What could change? They explore: hire editorial help, reduce frequency, change the nature of the content, reframe his personal relationship to "done." Multiple real options surface that weren't visible before naming the reality.

**Way Forward**: A specific commitment — not "I'll think about it" but a real next action with a time horizon.

What the demo shows: watching someone shift from anxiety-framing (boulder chasing me) to agency-framing (here's what I could actually do) in a 10-minute coaching conversation is the clearest argument for why GROW works. The shift is not the coach's insight — it's the person finding their own.

## Examples

**Without GROW (directing)**:
> "You should go talk to the engineering lead directly and get alignment before the next sprint."

**With GROW (coaching)**:
> "What outcome are you trying to get here?" (Goal)
> "What's made it hard to get there so far?" (Reality)
> "What approaches have you considered?" (Options)
> "Which one feels right to try first, and by when?" (Will)

The manager who uses GROW gets the same result but the team member walks away owning the solution.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM managing teams:

1. **Default to GROW in 1:1s when the person is capable**: Before jumping to advice, ask: can this person reach the right answer with guidance? If yes, use GROW. Reserve directing for genuine blockers, time-critical decisions, or when someone is clearly stuck and asking for input.

2. **Use GROW in performance conversations**: Start with their self-assessment, not your assessment. "What do you think is going well?" / "What do you think needs to change?" Most people know — they just need the space to say it. Your assessment lands more powerfully as a *response* to their self-assessment than as an opening statement.

3. **Apply "Will" seriously**: The commitment step is where GROW most often fails. Don't end a coaching conversation without a specific, time-bound next action the person states themselves. "I'll think about it" is not a Will. "I'll send a draft to the engineering lead by Thursday" is.

4. **For skip-levels and broader team coaching**: GROW works across hierarchical levels. Using it with PMs two levels below you sends a signal about how you want the culture to operate — problems get solved by the people closest to them.

5. **Combine with "enthusiastic rehire" for talent clarity**: When GROW isn't working — when you keep coaching the same person through the same problem without progress — that's a signal. Apply the "would I enthusiastically rehire?" test to determine whether this is a coaching problem or a fit problem.

6. **Pair with NVC for conflict coaching**: When the issue the person brings is a conflict with a peer or stakeholder, combine GROW with NVC framing. In the Options stage, introduce the "stay on your side of the net" principle — it often unlocks approaches the person hadn't considered because they were stuck in characterizing the other party.
