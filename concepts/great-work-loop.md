# Great Work Loop

## Definition

The **Great Work Loop** is Paul Graham's operating model for ambitious creative work: choose a field, learn enough to reach the frontier, notice gaps, and explore promising gaps. Curiosity drives the loop; successive versions compound it; morale and attention protect it.

It is a loop, not a plan. The field and the person coevolve as work proceeds, which is why the only reliable way to find the right work is to start working and keep following the parts that become more interesting.

## Why It Matters

Most organizations overmanage execution and undermanage problem choice. Graham's frame makes problem selection the core act of originality: people are often bolder in solutions than in choosing what to work on.

For product strategy, this is the difference between competing harder in a known lane and discovering the lane everyone else is ignoring. Great work often begins with a small, strange, unfashionable, or "toy-like" project that reveals a new frontier only after someone pulls on the thread.

## Examples

- **Toy-like starts**: An early version dismissed as a toy may have everything except scale. That can be a positive signal because scale can follow once the core is alive.
- **Unfashionable problems**: Problems that seem tapped out, boring, or beneath current fashion can hide latent potential. The lack of hype gives builders time and quiet.
- **Fresh eyes**: Newcomers notice awkward pieces of a field that experts have learned to ignore. If the issue still seems real after deeper learning, it may point to an undiscovered idea.
- **Small dedicated audience**: A tiny group that genuinely cares can sustain morale while the work is still illegible to the broader market.

## Actionable Practices

- Ask: **What am I excessively curious about?**
- Ask: **Does this field get more interesting as I learn more?**
- Ask: **What are people here too attached to?**
- Ask: **What becomes possible if that assumption is discarded?**
- Ask: **If I took a break from "serious" work to build something interesting, what would I build?**
- Start with **the simplest thing that could possibly work**.
- Make **successive versions** instead of overplanning.
- Protect contiguous work blocks and background thinking time.
- Treat setbacks as search data: backtrack only as much as needed, but do not abandon the root ambition in panic.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Run strategy reviews around gaps, not deliverables.** Ask each team to name the frontier they have reached, the gap they see, and why the gap is promising. A roadmap without a gap is execution theater.

2. **Fund curiosity-led probes.** Especially in AI products, the next durable advantage may start as a small internal tool, workflow hack, or disliked-by-reviewers prototype. Create space for small experiments whose only burden is that they reveal whether a thread is worth pulling.

3. **Use "toy" as a diagnostic, not a dismissal.** If a prototype is toy-like because it has a weak core, stop. If it is toy-like because it lacks scale but users feel the core, lean in. This links directly to [Tiny Core](../concepts/tiny-core.md).

4. **Protect senior attention as a strategic asset.** The loop requires background cognition. If the leadership system keeps all directors in reactive mode, it will select for incremental execution and against original problem choice.

5. **Hire for productive curiosity.** The strongest PM signal is not broad confidence; it is a person who can explain the specific question they cannot stop thinking about and the work they have already done to pull on it.
