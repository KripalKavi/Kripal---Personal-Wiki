## Definition

The people who were *best* at the old model are the *hardest* to reinvent — because they have no incentive to change, their employer affirms their current performance, and mastery of the old system creates a cognitive blind spot to the new one.

Coined by Nikhyl Singhal in the context of PM career transformation in the AI era: "The ones that were the best at working in the past find it hardest to go through this reinvention stage. The better you are at mastering one system, the less likely you are to recognize the new one."

## Why It Matters

Standard change management assumes resistance comes from underperformers or the disengaged. Shadow Superpower inverts this: **your highest-rated people are your biggest organizational risk in a paradigm shift.**

They face a convergence of factors that reinforce staying put:
1. **No personal incentive**: The current approach is working for them — promotions, comp, recognition
2. **Employer validation**: Their manager gives positive feedback for old behaviors; there's no signal that change is needed
3. **Cognitive anchoring**: Mastery of one system creates confidence that you understand the domain — and skepticism that the new thing is actually better
4. **Identity investment**: Years of work went into becoming excellent at the current model; accepting obsolescence means accepting that investment may not carry forward

The paradox: the weaker performers are *more* motivated to change because their current approach isn't working. High performers have every reason to stay.

## Examples

- An L66 PM who excels at synthesizing information, navigating stakeholders, and driving alignment across 20 teams. Under the new model, AI handles the information layer and judgment/building is what's valued. But they're rated 4/4 every cycle, so why change?
- A senior engineering manager who mastered influence through deep architectural review and code critique. Their entire identity and authority comes from that mastery — which is increasingly automatable.
- A strategy consultant who built a career on synthesizing information into executive presentations. The synthesis is now fast; the hard part is knowing what to do with it.

## PM Application

**For a Senior Director running a PM org:**

1. **Don't assume your top performers are leading the AI transition.** Run a separate calibration: who is actively building with new tools? High performance ratings and AI adoption are likely inversely correlated, not positively.

2. **Reframe resistance as a structural signal, not a motivation problem.** When a strong PM pushes back on AI adoption requirements, ask: "Is this person defending a superpower that's losing relevance?" vs. "Is this person raising a legitimate concern?" The answer changes the intervention.

3. **Create structured joy moments for high performers.** Their identity is invested in excellence — they need to experience being *excellent with new tools* before they'll commit to the transition. A hackathon, a "build something for yourself" 20% project, a show-and-tell where peers demo what they've built. The goal is the first joy moment, not a training mandate.

4. **Use skip-levels to probe for the pattern.** Direct asks: "What are you building right now?" "What did you automate this month?" "What's the most interesting thing you've done with AI tools?" The answers reveal whether a high-performing PM is on the right trajectory.

5. **Watch for Shadow Superpower in your manager layer.** PM managers who were promoted for information-movement and coordination skills may be inadvertently selecting for their own archetype in hiring and performance reviews — perpetuating the pattern one level down.
