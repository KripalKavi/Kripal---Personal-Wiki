# We Don't Sell Saddles Here

---

## Definition

A product positioning philosophy articulated by Stewart Butterfield in an internal Slack memo (2014): don't describe your product by what it technically IS, describe it by the transformation it enables for the customer.

The saddle metaphor: a saddle company that thinks of itself as selling saddles will optimize saddle features — materials, fit, durability. A saddle company that understands it sells horse riding, exploration, and freedom will make entirely different decisions about the product, the marketing, and the customer relationship.

The product is the vehicle. The transformation is the point.

---

## Why It Matters

Most products — especially in tech — are described and sold by their features and technical properties. This is a category error. Users don't want features; they want outcomes. The gap between "what the product does" and "what the user gets" is where most positioning fails and most roadmaps drift toward building for the wrong thing.

For Slack, the insight: they weren't selling messaging software. They were selling the feeling of a well-coordinated, fast-moving team — less email, less friction, more collective intelligence. That reframing changed how they prioritized features, wrote copy, and talked to customers.

The deeper implication: if you can't articulate the transformation your product enables in human terms (not product terms), you don't actually understand what your product is for.

---

## Examples

- **Slack**: Doesn't sell messaging → sells "less email, better team coordination, the feeling of a connected team"
- **Peloton**: Doesn't sell exercise equipment → sells discipline, community, and transformation of self
- **iPhone (original)**: Apple didn't sell "a phone with internet" → sold "the internet in your pocket, wherever you go"
- **Microsoft Copilot (what it should be)**: Doesn't sell "AI features in Office" → should sell "getting through your workday with more clarity, less friction, and time back for work that matters"

---

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **The Copilot positioning test**: Ask "what saddle are we selling?" for each Copilot surface. If your team describes Copilot by its features (AI summarization, chat interface, document drafting), you're selling saddles. Reframe: what transformation does the user experience? What does their workday feel like differently?

2. **Apply it to roadmap prioritization**: When two features compete for the roadmap, ask which one more directly enables the transformation — not which one is technically more impressive or easier to ship.

3. **Use it in executive communication**: Leadership, customers, and press understand transformations, not features. "Copilot saves you two hours a week on email" beats "Copilot uses AI to draft and summarize email."

4. **It's a product spec discipline**: If you can't write the "we don't sell saddles" version of your feature, you haven't understood the use case. Make this part of spec reviews.

5. **Test against it in user research**: After a user session, ask: did the user understand and feel the transformation you intended? If they can only describe what the product does, not how it changes their work, the positioning hasn't landed.

---

## Origin Story

Written as an internal memo when Slack had 8–10 people, before public launch, during private beta. Published verbatim — not edited before going public. Purpose: instill the ideas early enough that they could survive scaling.

The framing Butterfield credits to advertising tradition: if you want to sell Harley-Davidson, you're selling the open road, not the engine specs. But the product-strategy application — "you're not just creating the product, you're creating the market" — is his synthesis.

*Positioning* (Ries & Trout, 1981) is the underlying reference: almost impossible to create a new idea in someone's head. Easier to combine two existing ideas. If your product is different from alternatives in any significant way, you're building the category at the same time you're building the product. Those aren't separate tasks.
