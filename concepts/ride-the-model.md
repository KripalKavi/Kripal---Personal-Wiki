# Ride the Model

## Definition

**Ride the model** is Dan Shipper's survival principle for the AI era: the only durable strategy is to actively use each new model release on whatever you care about, the moment it drops, repeatedly turning over the same problems to see what newly works. The principle assumes the underlying *mechanism* of how models change the labor market:

> "What models do in general is they make yesterday's human competence cheap. And so it becomes commoditized. It's not valuable anymore. What humans do is we go in there and we're like, 'Yeah, we have all this frozen human competence from yesterday. How do I use this to make something new and interesting?'"

If you sit still, the model lapses your competence into the commodity layer. If you ride it, your competence stays one step ahead of the frozen layer.

## Why It Matters

Most "AI literacy" framings underspecify the action. They tell people to "learn AI" but not what learning looks like. Shipper's framing is operational:

- **It's not about understanding the model.** It's about *applying* the new model to your existing problems immediately.
- **It's not about prediction.** It's about *testing* — what could not be done last release, can it be done now?
- **It's not about San Francisco.** It's about wherever the model meets a real human doing something specific:

> "The edge of AI is wherever AI meets a real human doing something because the people at San Francisco, they're making it, but they don't actually know a lot about how to use it. They don't know, or at least they don't know everything about how to use it. They need to see how other people use it. And so whenever a new model comes out, you get to be one of the first people in the world to discover what it might be useful for."

The corollary: people working at *any* company, in *any* domain, who try every new release have a discovery surface the model labs themselves do not. Shipper's Every team is in Brooklyn, not SF, and his explicit claim is that they are ahead of SF on use cases precisely because they apply every release to actual work the next day.

## How To Actually Ride

1. **Build a small set of personal "rocks to turn over."** Problems that didn't work last release. Shipper's senior engineer benchmark is a literal example — every new model gets the same vibe-coded codebase with the same prompt: *"This is vibe coder slop. If you wanted to rewrite it from first principles, how would you rewrite it?"* All models before GPT 5.5 scored 30/100. GPT 5.5 scored 62. He doesn't predict the score; he turns the rock over.

2. **Try in your workflow first.** Not in a sandbox. Not in a demo. If the new release does not change how Codex/Cowork/Claude Code feels in your actual daily work in the first week, you haven't ridden it.

3. **Apply the [Reach Test](reach-test.md).** When you wake up tomorrow, do you reach for it organically? If yes, it's part of your workflow. If no, it isn't yet.

4. **Play. Have fun.** Shipper's specific framing: *"there's too much of 'I'm doing this because I have FOMO, I might lose my job.' ... The best way to actually figure out interesting, useful things to do with AI is to do something enjoyable."* The fear stance doesn't surface new use cases; the curiosity stance does.

5. **Don't wait for permission.** Shipper's complaint:

> "A lot of companies handicap their employees from even doing this because I don't know if you can use the latest models at Salesforce. A lot of times you have to wait or it's whatever. So maybe you have to do it in your off time."

Riding the model is a personal practice. If your employer slows you down, ride it on personal time.

## Examples

- **Marcus running Spiral at Every** — PM by background. *"He took a year off that job and just got super AI-pilled and just learned how to use cursor basically really well. Now I think he uses Claude Code, but he was extremely cursor pilled for a long time."* The combination — lightly technical + spiky product sense + always on the newest model — makes him faster than nearly anyone on the team.
- **Shipper's own senior engineer benchmark** — operational instance of turning over the same rock every release.
- **Shipper's email workflow** — when Codex got an in-app browser, he immediately moved his email into it. New capability → applied to existing problem within a week.
- **Every's hosted OpenClaw** — they rode the harness layer aggressively, ran into the limits ("way too much work"), and pivoted to a super-agent architecture. Riding produces *both* successes and informative failures; both feed forward.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Push the org to "ride the model" as a stated norm.** This is a cultural lever, not a tool rollout. The teams that ride compound advantage; the teams that wait fall behind. Make it visible: who tried the newest model this week? What did they discover that was not in the release notes?

2. **Insist on day-one internal access to frontier models — internal *and* external.** Shipper's complaint about Salesforce applies equally to Microsoft if Copilot teams have to wait weeks to use the latest Anthropic or OpenAI release. The lag is a strategic cost, not a security policy.

3. **The reviewer's question is "what did you try last release?"** When a PM presents Copilot strategy, the question that distinguishes signal from noise is what they *personally* tried in the most recent model drop. If they can't answer, the strategy is theoretical.

4. **Avoid the "wait until it's stable" trap.** "Wait until the model is stable enough for our customers" is a defensible position that becomes a slow-motion abandonment of the market. The competitive frontier rewards riding, not waiting.

5. **Pair with [Don't Be Fungible](dont-be-fungible.md) and [Cultivating Agency](cultivating-agency.md).** The career-durability question and the disposition question are the same answer at different layers: ride the model = exercise agency on the new capability = build a non-fungible combination.
