# Small Group Theory

## Definition

The world is run by group chats of eight people or fewer. Influence, taste, and decisions concentrate in small, selective coalitions. The product-strategy implication: it is sometimes correct — and even strategically necessary — to be **exclusive**.

[Max Schoening](../entities/max-schoening.md), in the contrarian-corner segment of his Lenny's appearance:

> "I think that inclusivity isn't always all that great. I think I very much believe in small group theory. I think the world is run by group chats of eight people or fewer. And so sometimes it's great to be exclusive."

---

## Why It Matters

The dominant Silicon Valley framing is *scale*: build for billions, default-open, broadest possible TAM. Small Group Theory cuts against this. Schoening's argument:

> "Notion could have the ambition to say, 'We are going to have eight billion users — every single person on the planet uses Notion.' And I think if we did that, we would very much upset the first, call it, 500 million, because the top of the class wants different things than everybody. And everybody is in the top of the class at something."

Two simultaneous claims:
1. **The top of the class wants something different from the average.** Designing for "everyone" produces a product that excels for no one. Average products lose to specific products.
2. **Everyone is in the top of the class at *something*.** This is the saving grace: exclusivity isn't elitism. You serve people-at-their-best, which is a different cohort for every domain.

---

## The TBPN Confirmation

Lenny adds a contemporary example: TBPN (the tech podcast). They had ~8,000 listeners and were acquired for hundreds of millions of dollars. Their own framing: *"If we have millions of people listening, we've done something wrong. This is specifically designed for the people in power of tech to influence them, to teach them what's going on."*

The exclusivity *is* the value proposition. A product that everyone consumed couldn't have done what TBPN did.

---

## The Hard Caveat

Schoening preempts the misreading:

> "I will have to caveat this with — if you're McDonald's and you have exclusive hiring practices and it's the only job in a location, that is not what I'm talking about."

Small Group Theory applies to **products and audience selection**, not to labor markets, monopolies, or essential services. The principle is *who you design for*, not *whose access you restrict to necessities*. Confusing the two produces moral-coded business decisions for non-moral problems.

---

## Why Small Groups "Run the World"

Schoening doesn't fully unpack this, but the underlying empirical claim is plausible:

- Decisions in companies, governments, communities are concentrated in trusted-group communications — group chats, Signal threads, dinner conversations — not broadcast channels.
- Taste, opinion, and consensus form first in small networks before diffusing.
- The "8 people or fewer" boundary is roughly the social bandwidth ceiling for trusted, high-context exchange. Above it, group dynamics degrade.

If true, then influencing the right 8-person group chat outperforms influencing thousands of people in broadcast channels. Products that serve that group chat — like TBPN — have outsized leverage.

## PM Application

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **The Microsoft default is the opposite of Small Group Theory.** Microsoft products historically target the largest possible enterprise and consumer markets. This is correct for some Microsoft products (Windows, Office) and wrong for others. The PM question per product: *what is the small group whose deep love this product needs?* If the answer is "all 1B knowledge workers," the product is likely undifferentiated.

2. **Copilot's small-group test.** Copilot for M365 cannot literally be small-group. But each Copilot *capability* probably can be: GitHub Copilot's loyal core (heavy developers); Copilot for Sales' loyal core (specific sales motions); Copilot for Security's loyal core (SOC analysts). Identify the small group whose obviously-good experience defines the product, even if the product is shipped to everyone.

3. **Stop optimizing for the mean user.** "Average user" research dilutes signal. Schoening's stronger frame: *"who is in the top of the class at the job this product is for?"* Design for them. The rest follow.

4. **Beware the inclusivity-as-strategy reflex.** It's politically safer to say "we serve everyone" than "we serve a specific group." Politically safer ≠ strategically right. Microsoft's brand is "for the world"; some Microsoft *products* benefit from being explicitly for someone smaller.

5. **The TBPN move at internal scale.** Inside Microsoft, the most influential opinions concentrate in small Signal/Teams group chats among senior PMs, partners, engineers. A *product strategy* that wins the right 8-person group chat inside the company will move faster than one that runs through formal channels. Likewise externally: which 8-person group chat is your product trying to win?

6. **Schoening's specific application to Notion**: serve the top 500M who *want different things*, not the universal 8B who would be alienated by serving the 500M well. The Microsoft Copilot equivalent: who are the 500M (or 50M, or 5M) for whom each Copilot surface is *unmistakably* the right product, and is it serving them, or has it diluted itself in pursuit of the universal use case?
