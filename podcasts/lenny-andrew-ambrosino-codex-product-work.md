## Summary

Andrew Ambrosino argues that AI has inverted product development: implementation is increasingly cheap, while deciding what is worth building, shaping it well, and knowing when the models are ready become the hard parts. The Codex team responds with high-agency builders, distributed “zone defense,” rapid prototypes, and a willingness to let promising features bake until model capability catches up.

## Key Takeaways

### 1. Taste becomes the bottleneck

When anyone can generate a working prototype, the scarce skill is judgment: knowing what should exist, how it should feel, which details matter, and when an idea is ready for users. Taste includes product judgment, systems thinking, and the ability to recognize quality—not merely visual polish.

### 2. Product work becomes zone defense

PMs need broader coverage and clearer ownership rather than dense coordination. Each product person owns a space, steers the work, fills gaps, and collaborates with engineers and designers who also have strong product instincts. The goal is not to eliminate roles but to make them less dependent on handoffs.

### 3. Prototype now; let capability catch up

The team keeps a portfolio of ideas alive, prototypes them, and revisits the ones whose viability depends on model intelligence. A feature that fails today may succeed unchanged a few months later when the model improves. Treating every failed prototype as a permanent product verdict throws away valuable optionality.

### 4. Timing can matter more than shape

Ambrosino believes the Codex app would have failed if launched in November rather than February, even with essentially the same product shape. Model capability and user readiness changed enough in that interval to alter the outcome.

### 5. Ambition must be calibrated

Teams can be too conservative, but they can also be “too AGI-pilled”—designing for a future model that users do not yet have. The right move is to be ambitious about the destination while grounding the current product in what the model can reliably do now.

### 6. A general work surface should orchestrate specialized tools

Codex does not need to replace every specialized application. It can act as a home base that understands the user's intent, invokes tools such as Excel or Premiere, and coordinates work across them.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **[Taste as Bottleneck](../concepts/taste-as-bottleneck.md)** - As AI makes implementation abundant, judgment about what to build and what good looks like becomes the constraint.
- **[Build and Bake](../concepts/build-and-bake.md)** - Prototype model-dependent ideas early, then let them mature until capability and market timing make them viable.
- **Zone defense** - Give product leaders distinct areas of ownership and coverage instead of making them co-managers of every decision.

## PM Relevance

1. **Change roadmap economics.** Maintain a shelf of prototypes for model-dependent opportunities instead of treating an early failure as a final no.
2. **Evaluate taste explicitly.** Hiring and performance reviews should test whether someone can recognize a strong product, simplify a messy idea, and raise the quality bar—not only execute a process.
3. **Reduce coordination tax.** Give high-agency PMs clear ownership zones and empower engineers/designers to make product decisions inside those zones.
4. **Separate ambition from readiness.** Keep the long-term product vision expansive, but gate launch decisions on current model reliability and user comprehension.
5. **Design Copilot as an orchestration layer.** The opportunity is not to rebuild every work application; it is to coordinate intent, context, and actions across the tools people already use.
