# 90 Minutes of Unfiltered Product Advice from Snap and Discord's Product Chief

## Metadata

- **Show**: Lenny's Podcast
- **Guest**: [Peter Sellis](../entities/peter-sellis.md), former first PM at Snapchat and Head of Product at Discord
- **Host**: [Lenny Rachitsky](../entities/lenny-rachitsky.md)
- **Date published**: 2026-09-20
- **Sources**: [Lenny's episode page](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/90-minutes-of-unfiltered-product), [PodScript transcript](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/lennys-podcast-product-career-growth/90-minutes-of-unfiltered-product-advice-from-snap-and-discords-product-chief-peter-sellis)

## Summary

Peter Sellis argues that great product organizations are built around strong ideology, explicit decision ownership, and a willingness to concentrate responsibility on unusually capable people. His product-growth lesson is sharper: return to the core value that makes users come back, improve it relentlessly, and only then earn the right to expand. The conversation connects taste, talent density, systems thinking, advertising economics, ambition, and the three productive tensions inside an excellent PM.

## Key Takeaways

### 1. Design teams for speed, not maximum collaboration

Sellis uses deliberately provocative language for a team with two properties: a shared ideology and a clear answer to who owns each decision. Collaboration has a hidden coordination cost; a system with too many shared decisions moves at the speed of its slowest node. The useful translation is mission alignment plus single-threaded ownership.

### 2. Make room for spiky talent

An organization that can only accommodate predictable, well-rounded performers is structurally unable to use some of its best people. Sellis describes managing Nikita Bier and other high-variance product leaders more like coaching distinctive artists: they may occasionally produce a dud, but the organization should preserve the conditions for exceptional work. Phil Jackson's ability to combine incompatible personalities is his leadership reference point.

### 3. The PM must create value over having no PM

Sellis's high bar for PMs comes from Snap's early operating model: the product should be good enough to operate without a PM, so the PM has to add judgment, taste, and leverage rather than ceremony. He argues that the best PMs often leave the profession to become founders or executives, which makes the median PM's value easy to overestimate.

### 4. Taste is partly the ability to say no

Sellis describes product leaders as museum curators: the product should expose only the small slice of the collection that is right for this time and place. A portfolio of good ideas that were deliberately not shipped is evidence of taste. This is especially relevant to AI products, where generation tends to add features and interfaces; PMs increasingly need to remove, constrain, and refuse.

### 5. Growth usually lives in the core product

At Snap, the post-2018 redesign recovery focused on performance, especially Android performance, rather than chasing new acquisition. At Discord, the team returned to its core job—being the best place to talk and hang out with friends before, during, and after multiplayer games—and improved the experience for that audience. Both examples suggest that DAU/MAU is a retention measure: a small improvement in habitual use can beat acquiring a large number of weakly retained monthly users.

### 6. Monetization must fit the product's physics

Sellis attributes Snap's advertising ceiling to three structural mismatches: a young audience with lower purchasing power, a camera-first interface with no natural ad slot, and a messaging/creation utility rather than a consumption feed. The lesson is not that the product failed—it built an exceptional consumer portfolio—but that product quality and monetization fit are separate questions.

### 7. Long-term greed is a practical operating principle

For ads, short-term revenue extraction can reduce the advertiser ROI that drives future budgets and retention. “Long-term greedy” gives teams an ideological cover for sacrificing near-term revenue when the sacrifice improves the compounding system. The principle is useful only when tied to a causal model of future value, not as a vague excuse to defer accountability.

### 8. Ambition is a muscle, and the target is acceleration

Sellis uses SpaceX sunsetting the exceptional Falcon 9 in favor of a heavier-lift system as an example of choosing the future over the current optimum. The product analogy is to optimize for acceleration toward the mission, not merely a favorable current position. AI lowers the cost of building, so teams need to deliberately practice making tomorrow unlike today.

## Notable Quotes

> “This person has to provide value over nobody.”

Sellis's test for whether a PM role is genuinely necessary.

> “If a museum has all of their collection on the walls, then the curator hasn't done anything.”

The role of taste as selection and restraint.

> “I don't want the position. I don't even want the speed. I want the acceleration.”

The ambition test: optimize for movement toward the mission, not just today's metrics.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **[Core Product Value](../concepts/core-product-value.md)** — a simple statement of the product's essential user value paired with causal metrics that explain why people return.
- **Spiky-talent organization** — build a system that can absorb unusual strengths and occasional misses without forcing everyone into the median.
- **Long-term greedy** — accept near-term revenue sacrifice when it compounds advertiser or user value.
- **Three PM oxymorons** — confidence with humility; comfort with ambiguity plus process discipline; long-term thinking plus urgent bias to action.

## PM Relevance

For a Senior Director of PM at Microsoft:

1. **Require every major product to state its core product value in one sentence and metrics.** The sentence should be memorable enough for onboarding; the metrics should be causal enough to guide prioritization.
2. **Audit coordination load.** For each decision class, name one accountable owner and define where consultation ends. Large cross-org programs often mistake more collaboration for better judgment.
3. **Protect high-variance talent without lowering the bar.** Give exceptional PMs larger decision surfaces, defend them publicly, and use private coaching when their judgment degrades.
4. **Make subtraction an explicit AI-era review criterion.** Ask what the team removed, refused, or simplified—not only what it generated.
5. **When growth stalls, test the core before adding a new surface.** Measure speed, reliability, repeat use, and task completion for the users who already care.
