# Stewart Butterfield

## Role / Background

Co-founder of Flickr (2004, sold to Yahoo) and Slack (2013, IPO 2019, acquired by Salesforce for $27.7B). Also co-founded Glitch, a game that pivoted into Slack. Born as Dharma Jeremy Butterfield; changed his name to Stewart at age 12. Trained as a philosopher before moving into tech — visible in his systematic, first-principles approach to product and org design. Rarely does podcasts; currently not running a new company; focused on family, philanthropy, and personal art projects.

## Key Ideas

- **[Utility Curves](../concepts/utility-curves.md)**: Features exist on an S-curve; investing below the threshold produces nothing. Most orgs add features that never clear the threshold, then conclude users don't want them. Also: divine discontent — the quality bar moves continuously, making shipped features slide back below threshold.
- **Comprehension vs. friction**: The real design challenge is usually comprehension ("what is this and what do I do?"), not friction. Most product design work is in this comprehension zone. Mantra: *Don't Make Me Think* (Steve Krug) — reduce cognition, not clicks.
- **Owner's Delusion**: Founders and PMs see their product through the lens of their investment and vision. They don't see it as a stranger with low intent. The restaurant website with Ken Burns effect and non-clickable phone numbers is the canonical example.
- **[We Don't Sell Saddles Here](../concepts/we-dont-sell-saddles-here.md)**: Position products by the transformation they enable. Written as an internal memo for Slack's 8-person team before public launch, verbatim. "You're not just creating the product — you're creating the market."
- **[Hyper-Realistic Work-Like Activities](../concepts/hyper-realistic-work-like-activities.md)**: Work that looks exactly like work but produces no value. The symptom of an organization with more headcount than known-valuable-work supply. Parkinson's Law applies: work expands to fill available time; headcount grows regardless of actual work supply.
- **Tilt your umbrella**: Empathy as competitive advantage. Most people are oblivious to their impact on others. "Your failure to really be empathic about other people's experience is an advantage that you can create." This drove Slack's word-of-mouth growth — small moments of delight created genuine advocates.
- **Product craft obsession**: "I feel like what we have right now is just a giant piece of shit... if you can't see almost limitless opportunities to improve, you shouldn't be designing the product." (MIT Technology Review, 2014). Perpetual dissatisfaction as a design culture.
- **The value creation chant**: At multiple company all-hands, had the entire company repeat: *"In the long run, the measure of our success will be the amount of value that we create for customers."* Generosity as game theory — cooperation signals build long-run trust and competitive advantage.
- **Pivot framework**: "Have you exhausted the realistic good ideas?" Pivoting is "fucking humiliating" — the emotional cost is why people persist too long. Requires cold rationality, not emotional decision-making. Referenced Annie Duke's *Quit* — smart fold when expected value drops below the next best alternative.
- **Specific craft examples**: Magic links (email → link instead of password), notifications default for new accounts (all messages until 10 received, then prompt), the shouty rooster (@everyone friction), Do Not Disturb rollout (elaborate permissions hierarchy to preserve existing setups).

## Relevance

Highest-signal product thinker in the wiki, with a rare track record of two category-defining products across different eras. His frameworks on org dysfunction (HRWLA, Parkinson's Law), product comprehension, and utility curves are directly applicable to large Microsoft product teams. His perspective on word-of-mouth through craft is particularly relevant for Copilot, where organic advocacy will be essential to enterprise spread.
